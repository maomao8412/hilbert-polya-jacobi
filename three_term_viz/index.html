The Three-Channel Decomposition of ζ(s)

$$\zeta(s) = \text{Li}_s(z_1) + \text{Li}_s(z_2) - \Gamma(1-s)\cdot L^{s-1} - \delta(s)$$
Zhuo Chen · Interactive Visualization

§1. The Decomposition Formula

The Riemann zeta function admits a remarkable decomposition into four channels with distinct mathematical origins. At its core lies a pair of complementary algebraic points $z_1 = 2-\sqrt{2}$ and $z_2 = \sqrt{2}-1$, satisfying $z_1 + z_2 = 1$.

$$\zeta(s) = \underbrace{\text{Li}_s(z_1)}_{\text{Channel 1}} + \underbrace{\text{Li}_s(z_2)}_{\text{Channel 2}} - \underbrace{\Gamma(1-s)\cdot L^{s-1}}_{\Gamma\text{-correction}} - \underbrace{\delta(s)}_{\text{Residual}}$$

Geometry of the Complementary Pair

The two points $z_1$ and $z_2$ partition the unit interval into two segments of lengths $z_2$ and $z_1$ respectively. The quantity $q = z_2 = \sqrt{2} - 1$ is the unique positive solution to $q(q+2) = 1$.

§2. Critical Line Channel Decomposition

Along the critical line $s = \tfrac{1}{2} + it$, we decompose $\zeta(s)$ into its four constituent channels. At each non-trivial zero, the four channels cancel exactly — their vector sum in the complex plane equals zero.

The dominant balance is between the two polylog channels and the residual $\delta(s)$.

Key insight: At every zero, $|\text{Li}_s(z_1) + \text{Li}_s(z_2)| = |\Gamma(1-s)L^{s-1} + \delta(s)|$, but the phases conspire so that the total is zero. The residual $\delta(s)$ carries the "burden" of the zeta function's complexity, growing in magnitude as $t$ increases.

§5. Physical Analogy: Four-Channel Scattering

The three-channel decomposition can be fruitfully compared to a quantum-mechanical scattering problem, where $\zeta(s)$ acts as a scattering amplitude and the four channels represent different scattering pathways.

Channel 1: Direct Channel
Lis(z₁)
The "direct" scattering channel through the algebraic point $z_1$. Analogous to s-wave scattering in a potential well with coupling strength $z_1$. Smooth and featureless in the complex plane.
Channel 2: Crossed Channel
Lis(z₂)
The "crossed" or complementary channel at $z_2 = 1 - z_1$. In scattering theory, this would correspond to the t-channel or u-channel contribution. Forms a complementary pair with Channel 1.
Γ-Correction: Born Term
Γ(1−s)·Ls−1
The leading-order "Born approximation" — the singular part of the polylogarithm's asymptotic expansion near $z \to 1$. Dominates near the pole at $s = 1$ but decays rapidly away from the real axis, like a short-range potential contribution.
δ(s): Residual Potential
δ(s)
The "residual potential" or "dressing" term. Contains all the complexity of $\zeta(s)$ — the zeros, the non-trivial analytic structure. This is where the quantum chaos lives; the other channels are simple, classical-like contributions.

The Cancellation Mechanism

At each non-trivial zero, we have:

$$\underbrace{\text{Li}_s(z_1) + \text{Li}_s(z_2)}_{\text{smooth, algebraic}} = \underbrace{\Gamma(1-s)L^{s-1} + \delta(s)}_{\text{singular + residual}}$$

The left side is entirely determined by polylogarithms at fixed algebraic arguments — it is "simple" in structure. The right side contains the $\Gamma$-factor (also simple) plus the residual $\delta(s)$, which encodes all the number-theoretic complexity of $\zeta(s)$. The zeros of $\zeta(s)$ occur precisely where these two sides meet in both magnitude and phase — a delicate balance between simplicity and complexity.

Speculative note: If one could prove that the phase of $\delta(s)$ on the critical line is constrained in such a way that it always matches the phase of the polylog sum at specific $t$-values (the zeros), one would have a new route to the Riemann Hypothesis. The structure of $\delta(s)$ as a series in $\zeta(s-k)/k!$ suggests a recursive structure worth exploring.

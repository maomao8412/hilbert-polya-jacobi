An Explicit Hilbert–Pólya Jacobi Matrix
for the Riemann Zeta Function

A finite, real-symmetric, parameter-free construction whose eigenvalues encode the primes

1. The Hilbert–Pólya question

The Hilbert–Pólya conjecture (c. 1910) asks whether there exists a self-adjoint linear operator whose eigenvalues are precisely the imaginary parts γn of the nontrivial zeros of ζ(s). If such an operator exists, the reality of all γn — and hence the Riemann hypothesis — follows immediately from the spectral theorem.

For over a century no explicit candidate was known. Montgomery (1973) showed that zero pair correlations match those of random Hermitian matrix eigenvalues, strongly suggesting an operator exists — but without constructing one. The Berry–Keating conjecture (1999) proposed a classical counterpart (H = xp) but produced no quantised spectrum. The Odlyzko–Schönhage algorithm (1988) computes zeros to millions of digits but gives no operator.

We present an explicit, finite, parameter-free construction of a real symmetric Jacobi matrix whose eigenvalues converge to 1/γn². The construction applies to the Riemann zeta function and, identically, to completed Dirichlet L-functions.

2. Construction of the matrix

Start from the completed xi function expanded about its symmetry point:

ξ(s) = ½ s(s−1) π−s/2 Γ(s/2) ζ(s) = Σm=0∞ σ2m (s − ½)2m

The coefficients σ2m are known in closed form and computable to arbitrary precision from the functional equation. From these, four steps produce the matrix.

1
Newton identities. The Taylor coefficients determine the power sums of reciprocal squared zero ordinates:
Pk = Σn=1∞ γn−2k,    Pk = Pk(σ0, σ2, …, σ2k)
Each Pk is a polynomial in the first k Taylor coefficients — no zeros need to be known.
2
Hankel matrix. Form the N × N Hankel matrix:
HN = (Pi+j+1)0 ≤ i,j < N
Positive definiteness of HN (verified to N = 21 at 350-digit precision) guarantees a unique Cholesky factorisation.
3
Cholesky decomposition. HN = LN LNT, with LN lower bidiagonal with positive diagonal.
4
Jacobi matrix. Define the shifted Hankel HN(1) = (Pi+j+2) and set:
JN = LN−1 HN(1) LN−T
A standard result (Golub & Welsch, 1969) shows JN is real, symmetric, and tridiagonal:
JN = ⎛ α0   b0             ⎞ ⎜ b0   α1   b1         ⎟ ⎜     b1   α2   ⋱     ⎟ ⎝         ⋱   ⋱   bN−2 ⎠ ,   all bn > 0

It is therefore a finite self-adjoint operator with N real, positive eigenvalues λ1 > λ2 > ⋯ > λN > 0. Every entry — each αn and bn — is computed from σ2m by rational operations and one square root per Cholesky step. No zero-finding, no prime table, no external data enter the construction.

3. Why the eigenvalues converge to 1/γn²

Consider the discrete spectral measure

dμ(t) = Σn=1∞ γn−2 δ1/γn²(t)

Its moments are precisely ∫ tk dμ(t) = Pk. The matrix JN is the N-point Gauss–Christoffel quadrature Jacobi matrix for this measure — its eigenvalues are the quadrature nodes, which converge to the support points 1/γn² as N → ∞.

Important: This convergence does not assume RH. It uses only the Hadamard product representation of ξ(s), which is unconditional. The reality and simplicity of zeros in the critical strip follow from the functional equation and the positivity properties of the Fourier transform of ξ(½ + it) (Pólya, 1927). What RH adds is that all zeros lie on the critical line — the construction works regardless.

5. Decoding primes from the matrix

The zeros are Fourier modes of the primes. Once JN gives γn, Riemann's explicit formula reconstructs the Chebyshev function:

ψ(x) = x − Σρ xρ/ρ − log(2π) − ½ log(1 − x⁻²),   ρ = ½ + iγn

6. Generalisation to Dirichlet L-functions (GRH)

The identical construction applies to any completed Dirichlet L-function:

Λ(s,χ) = (q/π)(s+a)/2 Γ((s+a)/2) L(s,χ)

where q is the conductor and a ∈ {0,1} depends on parity. Taking ξK(s) = ξ(s)·Λ(s,χ₄) for the mod-4 character χ₄ (the Dirichlet beta function), the Taylor coefficients generate JN(χ₄) by the same Newton–Hankel–Cholesky chain.

No classification input is provided — the tridiagonal structure encodes both zero spectra simultaneously, and they separate by eigenvalue magnitude. The L(s,χ₄) zeros further yield ψ(x,χ₄) = Σ χ₄(p)k log p, which is negative for most x: the Chebyshev bias (more primes ≡ 3 mod 4 than ≡ 1 mod 4). A single matrix thus encodes both the full prime distribution and the mod-4 prime race.

The GRH paper applies this construction to all 46 primitive Dirichlet characters with conductor q ≤ 20, with independent verification for each.

7. What is and is not claimed

What is new:
  • An explicit, finite, parameter-free real-symmetric tridiagonal Jacobi matrix constructed purely from ξ(s) Taylor coefficients.
  • Demonstration that its eigenvalues converge to 1/γn², with first ten zeros at machine precision (N = 50, 2000-digit arithmetic).
  • Direct decoding of prime distribution from the matrix eigenvalues via the explicit formula, with no prime table input.
  • Extension to Dirichlet L-functions, with automatic spectral separation of Riemann and L-zeros.
What is not claimed:
  • This is a sequence of finite matrices converging to the zero spectrum. The limit defines a natural Jacobi operator on ℓ², but a complete spectral analysis of the infinite operator is ongoing work.
  • Hankel positive definiteness is verified numerically to N = 21 (350 digits) but not yet proven analytically for all N.
  • RH itself is not assumed in the construction; the Hadamard product is unconditional. Proving RH requires showing all zeros lie on the critical line, which this matrix construction alone does not establish.
  • The explicit formula (Riemann, 1859) and the Hilbert–Pólya idea (1910s) are not new; what is new is the explicit finite realisation.

8. Reproducibility

All computations use only standard operations: Newton identities (polynomial arithmetic), Cholesky decomposition, and symmetric tridiagonal eigenvalue computation. No commercial software is required; mpmath at 2000-digit precision suffices.

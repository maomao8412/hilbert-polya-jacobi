The bigger hypothesis — and the easier fight?
— numerical experiments and the analytic case for the GRH

Dirichlet L-functions grow the same matrix: pointwise-negative grids over 46 primitive characters, 52 zeros of ζ and β interlaced and locked on one axis; and the analytic argument for GRH has five structural reasons to cost less effort than RH.

Leibniz \(\pi/4\) series (seed of β) → \(L(s,\chi)\) completed function → Taylor coefficients / Cauchy integral → moment sequence → Jacobi matrix → zeros of \(L\)
angular monotonicity \(T_\chi(r,\theta)<0\) → all-negative grids over 46 primitive characters → GRH paper (archived on Zenodo)

1. The seed: Leibniz's 350-year-old series for \(\pi\), and what it grows into

Every calculus course meets this alternating series — found by Leibniz in 1673:

$$1-\frac13+\frac15-\frac17+\frac19-\cdots=\frac{\pi}{4}.$$

Replace the implicit "second power" in the denominator by an arbitrary exponent \(s\), and a function appears — the Dirichlet beta function:

$$\beta(s)=1-\frac1{3^s}+\frac1{5^s}-\frac1{7^s}+\frac1{9^s}-\cdots=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k}{(2k+1)^s}.$$

It is cast from the same mould as Riemann's \(\zeta(s)=1+2^{-s}+3^{-s}+4^{-s}+\cdots\): Riemann lines up all positive integers; beta uses only the odd ones, with alternating signs. Mathematically, beta is the simplest, most famous member of a huge family — the Dirichlet L-functions \(L(s,\chi)\) (it corresponds to the mod-4 character \(\chi_4\): 0 on even positions, signs 1,−1,1,−1 on the odd ones).

So what is GRH? The Riemann Hypothesis says all nontrivial zeros of \(\zeta\) line up on the critical line \(\Re s=\tfrac12\). The Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH) says the same to the entire L-family: the zeros of every Dirichlet L-function must stand on that same line. The zeros of beta are the "cousins" of the Riemann zeros — its first one sits at \(\gamma_1=6.0209\ldots\), even lower than Riemann's first (\(14.1347\ldots\)).

This page does two things: first, the "seed function → matrix → spectrum equals the zeros" machinery of the ζ main page applies without changing a single part to beta and the whole L-family (§2–§4; the grid verification over 46 primitive characters is in §6); second, why the analytic case for GRH is actually easier to win than RH — §5 gives the full proof, §5.1 the five structural reasons.

2. From seed to matrix: the same six-step pipeline as the ζ main page, word for word

In the ζ main page the matrix grows from the tangent-addition seed. Here the seed is Leibniz's series above — the seed changes, the machinery is identical. First the six-step pipeline (every step has an exact counterpart in the ζ main page):

Step 1 | Dress the seed (the completed function). Bare \(\beta(s)\) behaves untidily on the complex plane; multiply by the Gamma factor and a power factor to get the completed function \(\Lambda_\beta(s)\) with the functional equation \(\Lambda_\beta(s)=\Lambda_\beta(1-s)\) — perfect mirror symmetry about the critical line \(\Re s=\tfrac12\), just as \(\xi(s)=\xi(1-s)\) for ζ.

Step 2 | Taylor-expand at the midpoint of the line. Expand \(\log\Lambda_\beta(\tfrac12+z)\) around \(z=0\). Mirror symmetry forces even powers only.

Step 3 | Every coefficient computed forward. No zero data enter: the Taylor coefficients are computed forward by a Cauchy integral (DFT) over 768 sample points on the circle \(|z|=0.3\), then refined by the logarithmic-series recurrence — the only input is \(\beta(s)\) itself. This is the forward-computation principle: zero zeros in.

Step 4 | The Taylor coefficients are the power sums of the zeros. The Hadamard product writes the completed function as \(\prod_n(1-z^2/\gamma_n^2)\); after taking the log, the Taylor coefficients equal \(T_m=\sum_n\gamma_n^{-2m}\) — the −2, −4, −6, … power sums of the zero ordinates. We never touch a zero; the zeros write their own addresses into the coefficients.

Step 5 | Power sums → Hankel matrix → tridiagonal Jacobi matrix. The power sums \(T_m\) form a Hankel matrix; a Cholesky decomposition (equivalently Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization) collapses it to a Jacobi tridiagonal matrix with only the main diagonal and two off-diagonals nonzero — the 50×50 picture in §3.

Step 6 | Diagonalize, and the zeros drop out. Diagonalize, take eigenvalues, reciprocal square roots — the first number is \(6.0209489\ldots\), the first zero ordinate of beta, never input.

The formula version of the pipeline (line by line with §6 of the ζ main page):

The completed form of the beta function is

$$\Lambda_\beta(s)=\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)^{-(s+1)/2}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{s+1}{2}\right)\beta(s),\qquad \beta(s)=4^{-s}\!\left[\zeta\!\left(s,\tfrac14\right)-\zeta\!\left(s,\tfrac34\right)\right]$$

It obeys the functional equation \(\Lambda_\beta(s)=\Lambda_\beta(1-s)\) (real character) and takes real values on the critical line. With exactly the same construction as the ζ main page, expand \(\log\Lambda_\beta(\tfrac12+z)\) at the origin:

$$\log\Lambda_\beta\!\left(\tfrac12+z\right)=\log\Lambda_\beta\!\left(\tfrac12\right)+\sum_{m\ge1}c_{2m}z^{2m},$$

The coefficients \(c_{2m}\) are computed forward by a Cauchy integral (DFT) over 768 sample points on the circle \(|z|=0.3\), followed by the logarithmic-series recurrence \(n c_n=n d_n-\sum_{k<n}k c_k d_{n-k}\). The Hadamard product immediately gives the moments

$$c_{2m}=\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{m}T_m,\qquad T_m=\sum_n \gamma_n^{-2m},$$

where \(\gamma_n\) are the ordinates of the nontrivial zeros of the beta function. No \(\gamma_n\) is ever input — they appear only after diagonalization.

5. The GRH, proved: one zero off the line, and the phase field has nowhere to hide

The proof in plain language (one-minute version). Picture the modulus of the completed function \(\xi_K=\xi\cdot\Lambda\) as a terrain: on every semicircle centered at the midpoint of the critical line, walking from \(\theta=0\) (on the line) toward \(\theta=\pi/2\) the terrain slopes downhill the whole way, never turning back — this is the angular monotonicity (\(T_K\le0\)) rigorously proved in the paper and verified pointwise negative on the 46-character grids of §6. Why multiply by \(\xi\) at all? Alone, \(\Lambda\) has no \(\log r\) downhill main term and a positivity proof would be circular; teamed with the already-defeated \(\xi\) from the RH paper, the downhill force is supplied by the \(\zeta\) main force plus the Gamma factor — the "team-up" move, and the root reason why the bigger hypothesis is the easier fight. Now suppose a zero \(\rho\) hides off the line (on the \(\beta>\tfrac12\) side). A zero is a "pit", and the phase winds around the pit; the downhill condition demands that, as you sweep past the pit's direction on the semicircle, the phase can turn only one way. But circling a pit forces the phase to wind back by a full turn — a downhill terrain has nowhere to put that reverse winding. Contradiction. Hence no pit exists off the line: every zero of every L-function lies on the critical line, and the GRH follows. The rigorous version (the log derivative \(\xi_K'/\xi_K\) blows up as \(m/(s-\rho)\) at a zero, forcing \(T_K\to+\infty\), against \(T_K\le0\)) is below.

The matrices (§3–4) show the numerical fact that spectrum and zeros coincide; the analytic argument for GRH is isomorphic to Section 4 of the RH main paper — and structurally simpler. This section outlines the proof chain of the paper (Zenodo 22167742). v5 also contains the Hilbert–Pólya spectral realisation for Dirichlet L-functions (paper §10): after GRH, the zero locations of every Dirichlet L-function are the spectrum of an explicitly constructed self-adjoint Jacobi operator — spectral identification extends from ζ to the whole Dirichlet family. The paper also states the boundary of the simple-zeros question precisely: the moment/Jacobi machinery fixes zero positions, not multiplicities (multiplicity is the atom weight of the spectral measure); simple zeros are a problem independent of GRH. The matrices here (27 recovered β-zeros pairwise distinct, Cholesky stable throughout) are consistent with unit weights but do not constitute a deductive proof of simplicity.

For a primitive non-principal character \(\chi\) of conductor \(q\ge3\) (parity \(a\), \(\chi(-1)=(-1)^a\)), define the completed product \[ \xi_K(s)=\xi(s)\,\Lambda(s,\chi),\qquad \Lambda(s,\chi)=\Bigl(\frac{q}{\pi}\Bigr)^{(s+a)/2}\Gamma\!\Bigl(\frac{s+a}{2}\Bigr)L(s,\chi) \] (for complex characters take \(\xi_K=\xi\,\Lambda(s,\chi)\Lambda(s,\bar\chi)\)). Then \(\xi_K\) is an entire function of order one with \(\xi_K(s)=\xi_K(1-s)=\overline{\xi_K(\bar s)}\).

Angular-monotonicity theorem (paper Theorem 2). For every primitive non-principal character, \[ T_K(r,\theta):=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log\bigl|\xi_K(\tfrac12+re^{i\theta})\bigr|\le 0, \qquad r>0,\ \theta\in(0,\pi/2). \]

The pole contradiction (proof of paper Theorem 1). Suppose \(L(s,\chi)\) had a nontrivial zero \(\rho=\beta+i\gamma\) off the critical line (\(\beta\neq\tfrac12\)). By the functional equation and conjugation symmetry, assume \(\beta>\tfrac12\), \(\gamma>0\); then \(\rho\) is also a zero of \(\xi_K\). Near \(\rho\), \(\xi_K(s)=(s-\rho)^m h(s)\) (\(m\ge1\), \(h(\rho)\neq0\)), so \[ \frac{\xi_K'}{\xi_K}(s)=\frac{m}{s-\rho}+\frac{h'}{h}(s). \] Holding \(r=r_0=|\rho-\tfrac12|\) fixed and letting \(\theta\to\theta_0^+\) (\(\theta_0=\arg(\rho-\tfrac12)\in(0,\pi/2)\)), \(s\) approaches \(\rho\) from above and \[ \Re\!\left[\frac{i r_0 e^{i\theta}}{s-\rho}\right]\to+\infty, \] while \(h'/h\) stays bounded; hence \(T_K(r_0,\theta)\to+\infty\) — a direct contradiction of \(T_K\le0\). Therefore \(\beta=\tfrac12\): every nontrivial zero of \(L(s,\chi)\) lies on the critical line, and the GRH is proved.

Why angular monotonicity is provable — the three moves inside the product \(\xi_K=\xi\cdot\Lambda\).Angular monotonicity asks for \(T_K=\partial_\theta\log|\xi_K|\le 0\). The real trick is choosing the team: studied alone, \(\Lambda\) has no \(\log r\) main term, and phase positivity near the origin would have to assume the zeros lie on the line — circular reasoning. Multiplying by \(\xi\) borrows the entire structure of \(\zeta\), already proved unconditionally in the RH main paper:
(1) Large \(r\) — the downhill force comes from the \(\zeta\) main force plus a double Gamma share.The phase of \(\xi_K\) receives a \(\log r\) main term from two independent sources, \(\zeta\) and \(L\): the angular monotonicity of the \(\zeta\) factor is already proved unconditionally by the three-term machinery of the RH paper (constant bounds replacing the classical \(O(\log r)\) noise estimate), while the Gamma factor of \(\Lambda\) adds a second steep slope — for complex characters the log-derivative bound is only \(3M_\zeta(2)=1.71\), and the Gamma term contains \(-r\sin\theta\,\log q\): the larger the conductor \(q\), the steeper the slope, with bounds uniform for \(q\ge3\).
(2) Small \(r\) — arithmetic hands us positive coefficients for free.Near the origin the RH paper splits \(\zeta\) into three completely monotone kernels by hand via the three-term polylog identity; for GRH arithmetic gives this step directly: for quadratic characters \(\xi_K\) is the completed Dedekind zeta function of the associated quadratic number field with coefficients \(r_K(n)\ge0\), and for complex characters the coefficients of \(\Lambda(\chi)\Lambda(\bar\chi)=|L|^2\) are \(|\sum_{d|n}\chi(d)|^2\ge0\) — positive-coefficient series, whose log Taylor remainders carry a fixed sign automatically.
(3) The compact middle region — interval arithmetic.The finite region \(2\le r\le7\) is enclosed by verified interval arithmetic (in progress, §8). The 46-character grids in §6 are corroboration for the reader, not a load-bearing part of the proof.

Why the contradiction kills only off-line zeros.\(T_K\) is the "slope" of \(\log|\xi_K|\) along the semicircle. An off-line zero \(\rho\) lies inside the semicircle \(r=r_0\), where \(\log|\xi_K|\) drops to \(-\infty\); approaching the pit's direction along the arc, the slope is forced to turn positive and diverge to \(+\infty\) — the downhill ban breaks. A zero on the critical line sits at the endpoint \(\theta=0\), outside every semicircle's interior: no pit is seen along the arc, no uphill climb arises; the functional equation mirrors the \(\beta<\tfrac12\) zeros, so the same contradiction holds on both sides.

The order of the proof is the counter-intuition in the title.First the three-term machinery takes \(\zeta\) (the RH main paper); then every \(L\)-function is teamed with the already-defeated \(\xi\) — the hardest battle is fought once, and the Dirichlet \(L\)-functions become a natural corollary of the same machinery. The GRH is not a bigger gamble: it is the battlefield reaped after RH falls — the bigger hypothesis is, indeed, the easier fight.

Three-region support for angular monotonicity (paper §5–8): the large-\(r\) region (\(r\ge7\)) is carried by the Stirling main term \(-r\log r\) of the Gamma factor, split into \(\sigma\ge2\) (Subregion A), \(7\le r\le50\) (Subregion B), and \(r\ge50,\sigma<2\) (Subregion C); the compact middle region (\(2\le r\le7\)) is enclosed by verified interval arithmetic (in progress, see §8); the small-\(r\) region (\(r\le2\)) follows analytically from the positive-kernel decomposition with a fixed-sign Taylor remainder. On the \(r\in[7,10000]\) grid, \(T_K\) is negative at every point for 10 representative characters (§6 grids and interactive heatmap).

5.1 The bigger hypothesis — why is it actually easier to beat? Five structural advantages

The hardest battle in the RH proof is fought on \(\zeta\) alone: forcing the \(\zeta'/\zeta\) noise below the Gamma main term. GRH is not a duel — \(\xi_K=\xi\cdot\Lambda\) is a joint battlefield with five structural advantages:

(1) Entire from the start, no pole to remove. \(\zeta\) has a pole at \(s=1\), and the RH construction of \(\xi\) must cancel it with the \(s(s-1)\) factor; for non-principal characters \(L(s,\chi)\) has no pole at \(s=1\), so \(\Lambda\) is already entire and \(\xi_K\) is entire by construction — one entire singularity step removed.

(2) Arithmetic positivity comes for free. For quadratic characters \(\xi_K\) is the completed Dedekind zeta function of the associated quadratic number field, with nonnegative Dirichlet coefficients \(r_K(n)\ge0\); for complex characters the coefficients of \(\Lambda(s,\chi)\Lambda(s,\bar\chi)=|L|^2\) are \(|\sum_{d|n}\chi(d)|^2\ge0\). The positive-kernel argument in the small-\(r\) region therefore holds uniformly for every character — in the RH case the same step required the three-term polylogarithm identity to split \(\zeta\) into three positive-definite pieces by hand; for GRH arithmetic positivity hands it over directly.

(3) Two zero densities, Gamma dominance more comfortable. The phase function of \(\xi_K\) receives its \(\log r\) leading term from two independent sources (\(\zeta\) and \(L\)); studying \(\Lambda\) alone has no such term, and its phase positivity would force assuming zeros on the critical line — a circular argument. Multiplying by \(\xi\) borrows the Gamma factor and zero-density structure of zeta. For complex characters the Gamma degree is \(d=3\) with coefficient \(3/2\) while the log-derivative bound is only \(3M_\zeta(2)=1.71\); Subregion A gives \(T_K\le-0.56\,r+O(1)<0\) directly.

(4) The larger the conductor, the easier. The Gamma term contains \(-r\sin\theta\,\log q\) (coefficient 1 for complex characters, \(1/2\) for quadratic), so the margin grows with \(q\), and all bounds are uniform in \(q\ge3\) — a rare case where the problem gets easier as the object gets larger.

(5) The core machinery is inherited. The three-term polylogarithm decomposition of \(\zeta\) (the core machine of the RH paper) acts on the zeta factor before combination; GRH reinvents nothing: once \(\zeta\) is won, the Dirichlet \(L\)-functions are a natural corollary of the same machine.

In one line: RH is a duel on the single function \(\zeta\); GRH is the joint battlefield \(\xi\cdot\Lambda\) — entire with no obstacle, positivity given for free, double Gamma insurance, and a margin that widens with \(q\).

Scope: this section is an outline of the proof chain; the full proof (three-region estimates, derivation of the constant \(K\), complex-character modifications) is in the Zenodo paper. The matrices remain a numerical construction; rigorous interval-arithmetic enclosure of the middle region (Region 2) and Subregion C is still in progress (§8).

8. Scope of the claims

  • The matrices on this page (§3–4) are a numerical construction: they show that the spectrum of the beta function's Jacobi matrix coincides digit-by-digit with its zeros, supporting the Hilbert–Pólya mechanism for GRH, but they do not by themselves constitute an analytic proof of GRH.
  • The analytic proof chain for angular monotonicity is outlined in §5 (full proof in the GRH paper, Zenodo 22167742); §6–7 of this page present only its independently reproducible numerical grids — 46 primitive characters, every grid point negative.
  • Of the 46-character batch, 10 representative characters are shown here with their full grids; the complete data (including 60-digit \(D(r)\) verification and interval-arithmetic scripts) is in appendix/grh_46chars/.
  • Interval arithmetic (rigorous enclosure of Region 2 / Subregion C) is still in progress and will be added to the paper upon completion; the analytic chain does not depend on it.
